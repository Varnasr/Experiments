Method and limits

The standards give, for each sex and each day of age, three numbers: the median M, the coefficient of variation S, and a power L that corrects for skew. A measurement y has the z-score ((y ÷ M)L − 1) ÷ (L × S). For height-for-age L is 1 and the distribution is normal.

For the three indicators that involve weight, WHO does not use that formula beyond three standard deviations. Past +3 or −3, the z-score moves one unit for each further step equal to the distance between the second and third standard deviation. This page does the same, and it reproduces the −4 and +4 columns of WHO's own tables.

Children under 24 months are compared on length, measured lying down, and older children on height, measured standing. A standing child is on average 0.7 cm shorter than the same child lying down, so a measurement taken the other way round is adjusted by 0.7 cm before use. Weight-for-length covers 45 to 110 cm and weight-for-height 65 to 120 cm. Outside those ranges no score is given.

Age in months is turned into days at 30.4375 days a month and rounded. A child's exact age in days from the date of birth is better when it is known. Rounded ages heap at 12, 24 and 36 months in most surveys, and that moves height-for-age.

A score is flagged as implausible, and left out of the prevalence, when it is outside WHO's limits: below −6 or above +5 for weight-for-age, below −6 or above +6 for height-for-age, and below −5 or above +5 for weight-for-height and BMI-for-age. A child with bilateral oedema is severely malnourished whatever the weight says, and this page has no field for it.

The page covers birth to 1,856 days. It does not hold the 2007 reference for ages five to nineteen, head circumference, or arm circumference. Prevalence from a pasted list is unweighted and has no confidence interval.

Source: WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group, WHO Child Growth Standards: Length/height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-length, weight-for-height and body mass index-for-age: Methods and development, World Health Organization, Geneva, 2006. The L, M and S values are from WHO's expanded z-score tables.
