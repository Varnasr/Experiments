Causal Inference WorkbenchDataResearch

Difference-in-differences, event studies, synthetic control and regression discontinuity: simulate a panel with a known effect, upload a CSV, or pull a live World Bank country panel and name the treated country.

World Bank API · cluster-robust SEs · wild cluster bootstrap · Goodman-Bacon decomposition · Sun–Abraham · placebo inference · IK bandwidth

First committed September 2026, last changed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. When a policy reaches some places and not others, the question is what would have happened without it. These four methods each build a counterfactual from the untreated units and measure the gap. The simulator plants a known effect so you can watch each estimator recover it, or fail when its assumption is violated.
  • DiD. Two-way fixed effects removed by alternating demeaning, OLS on the residualised variables by Cholesky, cluster-robust sandwich with the fixest small-sample convention, t on G−1 degrees of freedom. A wild cluster bootstrap p-value with the null imposed and Rademacher weights, over every sign pattern when there are 11 clusters or fewer. With staggered adoption, the Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition of the coefficient into its two-group comparisons and their weights.
  • Event study. Binned relative-time indicators with k = −1 omitted and a pre-period Wald test; the Sun–Abraham (2021) option averages cohort × relative-time coefficients with cohort-share weights and delta-method standard errors.
  • Synthetic control. Simplex weights by accelerated projected gradient with the exact projection of Duchi et al. (2008); placebo-in-space RMSPE ratios; an over-fitting warning when donors outnumber pre-periods.
  • Regression discontinuity. Local linear fits with a triangular kernel and HC1 errors, the Imbens–Kalyanaraman (2012) plug-in bandwidth, a bandwidth-sensitivity band, placebo cutoffs and a McCrary density test.
Verified: TWFE coefficient and SE match an explicit-dummies regression written separately in Python to six and four decimals; the wild bootstrap p-value matches a Python loop over every sign pattern and, up to its treatment of ties, the wildboottest package; the Bacon weights match weights computed from subsample variances and add back to the coefficient; the RD simulator's interval covers the planted jump.
Known limits. The regression discontinuity interval is the conventional one: the bias-corrected interval of Calonico, Cattaneo and Titiunik is not implemented, and the McCrary density test is a simplified version on binned counts. Standard errors are clustered at the unit level. The wild cluster bootstrap gives a p-value and no interval, and with G clusters it cannot go below 2/2G. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition needs a balanced panel, treatment that switches on once and stays on, and no unit treated from the first period; it has no covariates. Synthetic control uses pre-period outcomes as its only predictors, and when donors outnumber pre-periods the weights are not unique and the placebo p-value should not be read. The World Bank panel shows the mechanics and supports no causal claim by itself.

Poverty & Inequality ExplorerDataResearch

What POVCAL did with grouped data: Lorenz curves fitted to decile shares pulled live from the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform, then Gini, Theil, Atkinson, Palma and FGT poverty at any line, the Datt–Ravallion decomposition and a growth incidence curve.

PIP API · GQ and Beta Lorenz curves · Shapley decomposition

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Published inequality data usually comes as ten numbers, the share of income going to each tenth of the population. From those ten numbers and the average, this page reconstructs the whole distribution and reads off how many people fall below any poverty line, how unequal the country is, and whether growth between two surveys reached the poor. One click compares its answers with the World Bank's own.
  • Lorenz curves after Datt (1998): General Quadratic and Beta forms fitted by OLS to the cumulative decile points, validity checked on a 2,000-point grid and against the theoretical conditions; the valid curve with the smaller squared error wins.
  • Measures from the fitted quantile function y(p) = μ·L′(p) on a 4,000-point grid: Gini, Theil T, mean log deviation, Atkinson at three aversions, Palma, P90/P10, median; headcount by bisection, FGT1 and FGT2 by integration, Watts.
  • Change over time: Datt–Ravallion growth and redistribution with its residual, the Shapley version without one, and the Ravallion–Chen growth incidence curve.
Verified: on India 2022 the page gives Gini 0.256 and a $3.00 headcount of 5.1% against PIP's microdata 0.255 and 5.3%; the GQ results match Datt's closed forms to four decimals.
Known limits. Ten grouped points determine three parameters, so the fitted curves are least reliable in the tails, and the Atkinson(2) index and the ends of the growth incidence curve depend on those tails. PIP labels each survey as income or consumption and as national, urban or rural; an income survey and a consumption survey should not be compared as if they measured the same thing.

Multidimensional PovertyDataResearch

The Alkire–Foster index from your own file: one row per person, a 0 or 1 for each deprivation, your weights and cutoff. Headcount, intensity and the adjusted headcount; what each indicator and each group contributes; the shares who are vulnerable and in severe poverty; and the same figures at other cutoffs.

Alkire–Foster M₀ · censored headcounts · subgroup decomposition · cutoff sensitivity

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. A household can be above an income line and still have no toilet, no school-going child and a malnourished one. This page counts people who are deprived in several such things at once, says how deprived they are, and shows which deprivations and which groups account for most of the total.
  • A person's score is the weighted sum of their deprivations, with weights rescaled to add to one; poor at or above the cutoff k; M₀ = H × A (Alkire and Foster 2011).
  • Indicator shares from censored headcounts, which weighted and summed give M₀; group shares from population share times the group's M₀.
  • A score that reaches the cutoff only up to floating-point rounding counts as reaching it, so 0.7 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 meets a cutoff of 1.
Verified: a four-person case worked by hand; every figure on the simulated example against a Python calculation in exact fractions, for three regions and five cutoffs; both decomposition identities hold.
Known limits. Each 0 or 1 is taken as given: building the indicators from a survey is the hard part and happens before the file is made. A row with a missing indicator is left out, where the global index applies its own rules. There are no standard errors, so a gap between small groups may be noise. Only M₀ is computed. The results have not been compared with a published national index, and the example describes no country.

Wage Gap DecompositionWomenResearch

Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition of a wage gap on your own microdata: how much is education, experience, sector and location, and how much is the same characteristics being paid differently. Four reference-coefficient choices, three-fold form, bootstrap standard errors.

HC1 regressions · Jann (2008) pooled reference · bootstrap

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Women earn less than men in every labour force survey India has run. This page splits the gap into the part that comes from differences in what women and men bring to the market and the part that comes from how the market pays them for it, and shows how much the answer depends on choices that are usually hidden.
  • Two-fold: (X̄A − X̄B)′β* + [X̄A′(βA − β*) + X̄B′(β* − βB)], with β* from the pooled regression with a group dummy (Jann 2008), the pooled regression without it (Neumark 1988), or either group.
  • Three-fold: endowments, coefficients and interaction. Detailed contributions per variable, with the Oaxaca–Ransom warning that dummy-variable rows depend on the base category.
  • Bootstrap standard errors resample workers and rerun both regressions and the decomposition each time; text columns are expanded to dummies against their most common category.
Verified: totals reproduced exactly by an independent plain-Python implementation on the same simulated sample.
Known limits. It decomposes the gap in mean wages and says nothing about the gap at other points of the wage distribution. The unexplained contribution of each dummy variable depends on which category is the base (Oaxaca and Ransom 1999); the totals do not. Rows with a missing value are dropped, with a count.

Fiscal IncidenceDataResearch

Who pays a tax and who receives a transfer, from a table of income groups: the concentration coefficient and Kakwani index of each instrument, the fall in the Gini split into vertical equity and reranking, and how many Gini points each tax or transfer accounts for with the others in place.

Kakwani · Reynolds–Smolensky · Atkinson–Plotnick reranking · Lambert identity · marginal contributions

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. A subsidy or a tax has a total in the budget, and it also falls on some people more than others. Type in what each income group pays and receives, and the page says whether each item favours the poor or the rich, how much the whole set narrows the gap between them, and which item does most of the narrowing.
  • Groups are ranked by income before taxes and transfers. A Gini or a concentration coefficient is one minus twice the area under the curve through the group points, weighted by population.
  • Kakwani index CT − GX for a tax and GX − CB for a transfer; redistributive effect GX − GN, split into vertical equity and reranking; vertical equity printed a second time from (t·KT + b·KB)/(1 − t + b).
  • The marginal contribution of an instrument is the Gini of final income without it, less the Gini with it; the page flags an instrument whose Kakwani index and marginal contribution disagree.
Verified: every coefficient on the example matches a Python calculation by a different formula (mean absolute difference, covariance with the mid-rank) to ten decimals; Kakwani's and Lambert's identities hold; a case built to rerank gives 0.25, 0.13 and 0.08.
Known limits. Everyone in a group is treated as having the group's mean income, so every Gini is lower than microdata would give and reranking inside a group cannot be seen. The table is taken as given: who bears an indirect or a corporate tax is an assumption made before it is filled in. There is no behavioural response. Services such as schooling and health care count only if they have been valued and entered as transfers. The results have not been compared with a published incidence study, and the example describes no country.

Robustness ValueResearch

How strong a left-out variable would have to be to overturn a regression estimate. From a coefficient, its standard error and the degrees of freedom: the Cinelli–Hazlett robustness value, the partial R² of the treatment, and the estimate that would remain if the confounder were as strong as a covariate you name.

Cinelli and Hazlett (2020) · partial R² · bounds from a benchmark covariate

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Every regression that claims a cause leaves something out. This page does not ask whether something was left out. It asks how much the left-out thing would have to explain, of both the treatment and the outcome, before the result disappears, and it lets you compare that with a variable you did measure.
  • The robustness value is ½(√(f⁴ + 4f²) − f²) with f = q|t| ÷ √df. At a significance level, f is reduced by the critical t at df − 1 over √(df − 1), and the second bound of the 2020 paper takes over when the estimate is precise enough for it to bind.
  • Student's t by the continued fraction for the incomplete beta function, and its quantile by bisection.
  • A confounder k times as strong as a covariate is turned into two partial R² values with the bounds in section 4.4 of the paper, and then into an adjusted estimate, standard error, t and interval.
Verified: the Darfur example returns the 13.9%, 7.6% and 2.2% the paper prints and its adjusted estimate of 0.0752 with interval 0.032 to 0.118; t quantiles match scipy to nine decimals; the bounds match a second implementation in Python.
Known limits. One linear regression coefficient with conventional standard errors. With clustered or robust errors the page uses the t-statistic and degrees of freedom you enter and makes no further adjustment. It does not cover instrumental variables, logit or probit. The robustness value describes the estimate and says nothing about whether such a confounder exists. Oster's δ is not computed.

Women in the NFHSWomenData

Women's health, work, assets, agency and safety across India's states and the five NFHS rounds, pulled live from the DHS Program API: state rankings with change since the previous round, and who is behind by wealth, residence, education and age.

DHS API · dumbbell charts · approximate design-based intervals · CSV export

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Pick anaemia, bank accounts, spousal violence, child marriage or any of twenty indicators and see every state's number, how far it moved since the last survey, and whether the poorest or least educated women are the ones still behind. Nothing is typed in by hand: every figure comes from the survey's own API with its sample size.
  • Pulls api.dhsprogram.com/rest/dhs/data for India, all rounds, all breakdowns, and resolves NFHS-5's nested regions and renamed states before matching across rounds.
  • The API carries sample sizes but not NFHS's published confidence intervals, so the page draws Wilson intervals on the unweighted n inflated by a design effect the reader sets, and labels them approximate.
  • Says when a module has no all-India total in the API (domestic violence) rather than inventing one.
Verified: anaemia among women renders 57.0% for NFHS-5 and 53.1% for NFHS-4, matching the published fact sheets.
Known limits. The DHS API does not carry the design-based confidence intervals that NFHS publishes in its reports. The intervals drawn are Wilson intervals on the unweighted sample size, inflated by a design effect the reader sets (1.5 by default), and they are approximate. Some modules, the domestic violence module among them, have no all-India total in the API.

Heat and Outdoor WorkClimateWomen

Hour-by-hour heat stress for outdoor workers from live weather: WBGT for any place in India this week, the occupational work–rest limits by workload, safe working hours per day, wages lost, and how the count of dangerous-heat days has changed since 1991.

Open-Meteo forecast and ERA5 archive · ACGIH screening limits · OLS trend

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Heat takes hours and wages from outdoor workers long before it puts anyone in a clinic. The people who work through an Indian summer, on farms, at kilns, on construction sites and on public-works schemes where women are most of the workforce, lose hours and wages as the air gets hotter and wetter. This page says which hours are safe to work this week, what the lost hours cost, and whether the summers are getting worse where you are.
  • WBGT in the sun from the Australian Bureau of Meteorology approximation (0.567·T + 0.393·e + 3.94); in shade and at night from Stull's natural wet-bulb, 0.7·Tw + 0.3·T.
  • Each hour gets the largest work allocation the ACGIH threshold limit values (acclimatised) or action limits (unacclimatised) permit for the chosen workload; hours lost are summed over the workday and priced at the wage you enter.
  • Thirty-five years of daily maxima and mean humidity from the ERA5-based archive give March–June counts of days over the continuous-work and almost-no-work limits, with a least-squares trend and its 95% interval.
Verified: 35 °C at 50% humidity gives WBGT 34.8 °C, matching the Bureau of Meteorology's tables; the allocation function returns the ACGIH table's values.
Known limits. The wet-bulb globe temperature in the sun is an approximation that assumes moderately high radiation and light wind, so it is a screening value and no substitute for a measurement on site. Reanalysis cells are roughly 10 km wide, so a city's series is an area average and understates the urban heat island. The work limits are for healthy, hydrated, lightly clothed adults and do not cover pregnancy, chronic illness or protective clothing.

Unpaid Care ValuationWomenData

What unpaid domestic and care work is worth at market wages, for women and men in every Indian state. Minutes a day from the Time Use Survey, casual and regular wages from the Periodic Labour Force Survey, for 2024 and for 2019, with the two years set side by side in 2024 prices and every step from wage to hourly rate left open to change.

TUS 2024 unit-level data · TUS 2019 report · PLFS wages · CPI · replacement cost

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Women in India spend about five and a half hours a day cooking, cleaning and caring for others without pay, and men about forty minutes. This page asks what those hours would cost if someone were paid the going wage in that state to do them, and shows the answer for one person for a day and for a year.
  • Minutes are per person per day for ICATUS major divisions 3 and 4, with a time slot shared equally among the activities reported in it, computed from the unit-level data with the survey multipliers for 36 states and union territories.
  • The hourly rate is the casual daily wage over the hours the same workers reported on a casual day, or the regular monthly wage over their hours in the survey week times 52 ÷ 12, both from the PLFS unit-level data. Value is minutes ÷ 60 × rate, and × 365 for a year. A wage from fewer than 30 workers in the sample is not used.
  • One wage for both sexes by default. Each sex at its own wage is an option, and it builds the wage gap into the value.
  • For 2019 the minutes and their standard errors are read from the survey report, and the wages are computed from the PLFS unit-level data for January to December 2019. 2019 rupees are restated with the all-India CPI. A change in minutes is marked clear at 1.96 standard errors.
Verified: the all-India minutes computed from the unit-level data equal the eight figures MoSPI publishes; every wage on the page equals the series as read, and the regular wages recomputed from the unit-level data agree with them to 0.02%; the valuation is worked by hand. For 2019: the all-India minutes equal the four MoSPI publishes, the wages computed by quarter agree with MoSPI's to 0.15% for regular and 1.75% for casual work, and the comparison is checked against values worked in Python.
Known limits. A generalist replacement cost: care of a child is priced like sweeping. Small states rest on few households, and the standard errors shown ignore stratification. Eighteen casual-wage cells and two regular-wage cells rest on fewer than 30 workers and give no value: the published casual wage for women comes from one woman in Chandigarh and one in Mizoram, and Lakshadweep has none. A regular employee's hours are those in all work in the survey week, which includes any second job. No state total is given, because that needs population by age. The 2019 unit-level file has no times in it, so the 2019 minutes are the report's, in whole minutes, with the survey office's standard errors, which are larger than the ones computed here for 2024; the test of a change mixes the two. The 2019 wage cells count every urban visit and the 2024 cells the first visit only, so a state's regular wage can move with the sample. One national price index is used for every state.

RCT PlannerToolResearch

Power and minimum detectable effect for individual and cluster designs, with intra-cluster correlation, covariate adjustment, attrition, take-up and multiple outcomes; then a seeded, stratified randomiser for a baseline CSV with a balance table and a randomisation record.

Bloom / Duflo–Glennerster–Kremer formulas · permuted blocks · Welch and joint F balance tests

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Before a trial starts, two questions: how small an effect this sample could detect once villages cluster, some people drop out and not everyone takes the programme up; and who gets treatment. The page answers the first with curves you can move, and does the second with a draw that is stratified, reproducible from a seed, and documented for the pre-analysis plan.
  • MDE = (t1−α/2 + t1−β)·√[1/(P(1−P))]·√[σ²(1−R²)·(1+(m−1)ρ)/N], t quantiles from the regularised incomplete beta function; required sample by bisection.
  • Randomisation within strata by permuted blocks with a seeded generator, allocation remainders carried across strata so small strata still add up to the target split, cluster-level draws supported.
  • Balance table with Welch p-values and a joint F-test; a randomisation record with the seed, algorithm and a SHA-256 digest of the input file.
Verified: MDE 0.177 for N = 1,000 matches the closed form; 18/18 cluster splits and 40/30/30 three-arm splits come out exact.
Known limits. The power formula assumes clusters of equal size and one level of clustering. Several primary outcomes are handled by Bonferroni, which is conservative when the outcomes are correlated. Attrition is applied as one share of the whole sample. The balance table tests one covariate at a time, so about one in twenty will differ at the 5% level by chance.

Sampling DesignToolResearch

Sample size for a survey estimate or a change between two rounds, with design effect, finite population correction and non-response; proportional, Neyman and equal allocation across strata; then a seeded draw of clusters with probability proportional to size from your own frame, with the weight each interview carries and a sampling record.

Cochran · Kish design effect · Neyman allocation · systematic PPS · SHA-256 record

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. A survey that describes a population needs three decisions before fieldwork: how many households to interview, how to divide them between places, and which villages to go to. The page works each one out with the arithmetic shown, and draws the villages so that larger ones are more likely to be chosen and the draw can be repeated from its seed.
  • n₀ = z²p(1−p)/e² or z²σ²/e², corrected for a finite population, multiplied by 1 + (m−1)ρ and divided by the response rate; for a change between two rounds, (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² times the summed variances over the squared difference. Normal quantiles by bisection on the power series of the distribution function.
  • Allocation in proportion to Nh, to NhSh (Neyman) or equally, with a minimum and the stratum population as bounds, rounded by largest remainder, and the standard error of the stratified mean under each.
  • Systematic PPS in file order, with certainty selections removed and the interval recomputed; a start for each stratum from a seeded generator; weights as the inverse of the two-stage probability; a record with the seed and a SHA-256 digest of the frame.
Verified: sizes, allocations and standard errors match values computed separately in Python (385 for p = 0.5 and e = 0.05; 833 with a design effect of 1.95 and 90% response); the draw matches a second implementation over several hundred random frames, selection frequencies match k·Mi/M, and the weights add back to the frame total.
Known limits. The design effect 1 + (m−1)ρ assumes clusters of equal size and leaves out the loss of precision from unequal weights. The finite population correction is applied before the design effect; some texts apply them in the other order. A comparison between two rounds treats them as independent samples, so it overstates what a panel of the same households needs. Allocation is for one outcome. Systematic selection gives no joint selection probabilities, so variance has to be estimated by the with-replacement approximation. The page draws clusters; listing and choosing households within one is fieldwork.

Multiple Outcomes & Attrition BoundsToolResearch

Two checks on a trial's results. P-values for a family of outcomes adjusted by Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg and sharpened q-values, with a count of what still stands. And Lee and Manski bounds on the effect when the two arms lose different shares of their sample, with bootstrap intervals.

Holm · Benjamini–Hochberg · Anderson sharpened q · Lee bounds · Manski bounds

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Test twenty outcomes and one will look like a success by luck alone. The first tab says which results survive once the number of tests is allowed for. The second asks what happens if the people who dropped out of one group differed from those who dropped out of the other, and gives the range the true effect could lie in.
  • Holm by a running maximum of p·(M − rank + 1), Benjamini–Hochberg by a running minimum of p·M/rank, and the sharpened q-value as the smallest level on a 0.001 grid at which the two-stage Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli procedure rejects.
  • Lee bounds trim the arm that kept more people by the difference in response rates over the higher rate, with a fractional weight on the observation at the cut; Manski bounds fill missing outcomes with the lowest and highest possible values.
  • Intervals by a seeded percentile bootstrap that resamples people within each arm with their missing outcomes.
Verified: Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg match statsmodels on three families; sharpened q-values match a separate Python routine; the Lee bounds match cases worked by hand and a Python routine by a different method on the simulated example, where they contain the true effect.
Known limits. The adjustments take p-values as given and ignore how the outcomes are correlated, so Holm is conservative when they move together; Westfall–Young and Romano–Wolf need the data. Leaving tested outcomes off the list makes every adjusted value too small. The Lee bounds assume treatment moves people only into response or only out of it, which cannot be tested here, and use no covariates to tighten them. The bootstrap interval is wider than the Imbens–Manski interval and is wrong for a cluster-randomised trial, because it resamples individuals.

Survey Data AuditorDataTool

The checks a monitoring team runs before anyone estimates anything: duplicate and near-duplicate interviews, Benford's law, age heaping, straightlining, interview length and timing, missingness, and an enumerator profile that says who to call back.

Nigrini MAD · Whipple and Myers · Welch z-scores · Markdown and CSV export

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Field surveys go wrong in predictable ways: an interviewer copies yesterday's form under a new number, invents incomes, rounds every age to a five, answers a whole block with the same tick, or finishes a forty-minute interview in eight. Drop in the CSV and the page finds each pattern, names the enumerator, and writes the call-back list.
  • Exact duplicates by hashing every column except ID and timestamps; near-duplicates by pairwise comparison within an enumerator at 90% identical fields.
  • Benford judged by Nigrini's mean-absolute-deviation cut-offs, per-enumerator by χ² p-value because MAD is unreliable at small n; Whipple's index on the UN scale and Myers' blended index; straightlining against a chance benchmark.
  • Every metric z-scored across enumerators; two flags puts a name on the list. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.
Verified: the seeded demo plants six problems across five enumerators and the audit recovers all six, attributed to the right people.
Known limits. Every verdict is a threshold on a statistic. A flag is a reason to call back and proves nothing about an enumerator. Column roles are guessed from header names and value patterns and need checking before the report is read. Benford's law is offered only for amounts whose 5th to 95th percentile spans at least fifty-fold, because it does not apply to bounded numbers.

Cost–Benefit CalculatorToolResearch

Net present value, benefit–cost ratio, IRR, payback and cost per outcome for a programme's cost, benefit and outcome streams, with Monte Carlo on every input and a tornado chart of which assumptions drive the answer.

Triangular draws · bisection IRR · one-at-a-time tornado

First committed September 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. Every donor asks whether a programme was worth it; almost nobody shows how sure they are. Enter what it costs, what it returns and how confident you are of each, and the page gives the usual numbers plus the probability the programme pays off and the two or three assumptions on which that hangs.
  • PV by year with year 0 undiscounted; IRR by bisection with a warning when net flows change sign more than once; cost per outcome shown with outcomes undiscounted and discounted.
  • Monte Carlo draws each stream from a triangular distribution on its range and the discount rate uniformly; the tornado sets each input to its low and high with everything else fixed.
  • Streams are independent by design, and the page says what that leaves out.
Verified: PV, NPV, BCR, IRR and cost per outcome on the illustrative example reproduced to the cent in an independent Python calculation.
Known limits. The simulation treats cost and benefit streams as independent, so inputs that move together would give a wider distribution than the one shown. Benefits are not weighted by who receives them. When the net flow changes sign more than once, the internal rate of return may not be unique.

Child Growth z-scoresDataResearch

Height-for-age, weight-for-height, weight-for-age and BMI-for-age for children under five, against the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards. One child at a time, or a pasted list with the share stunted, wasted and underweight.

WHO 2006 LMS tables · restricted method beyond ±3 SD · implausible-value flags

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. A child's weight and height mean little without knowing what healthy children of the same age and sex weigh and measure. This page places a child against the World Health Organization's standard and says how far below or above the middle the child is, in the unit nutrition surveys use.
  • z = ((y ÷ M)L − 1) ÷ (L × S) with L, M and S for each day of age from 0 to 1,856 and each 0.1 cm of length or height, from WHO's expanded tables.
  • Beyond three standard deviations the weight-based indicators use WHO's rule: one unit for each further distance equal to the gap between the second and third standard deviation.
  • Length under 24 months and height from 24, with 0.7 cm added or taken off for a child measured the other way. Scores outside WHO's plausibility limits are flagged and left out of prevalence.
Verified: for four rows of each of WHO's ten tables, all nine printed values from −4 to +4 standard deviations return their own number to within 0.01, 360 values in all; five children match a second implementation in Python.
Known limits. Birth to five years only: the 2007 reference for ages 5 to 19, head circumference and arm circumference are not included. There is no field for oedema, and a child with bilateral oedema is severely malnourished whatever the weight. Age entered in months is converted at 30.4375 days a month. Prevalence from a list is unweighted and has no confidence interval.

Rupee ConverterData

Restate a rupee amount at the prices of another month, calendar year or financial year with the all-India Consumer Price Index, and move between rupees and 2021 PPP dollars, the unit of the World Bank's poverty lines.

MoSPI CPI, base 2012 joined to base 2024 · World Bank 2021 PPP

First committed November 2026.

Open the tool →
In plain words. A thousand rupees in 2013 bought what nearly two thousand buys now. This page does that sum for any two months or years since 2013, and it turns rupees into the international dollars in which global poverty lines are written.

Method

Every tool can be read in full, and every result is checked against something outside the tool.

  1. Compute in the browserCholesky solves, alternating-projection demeaning, cluster-robust sandwiches, simplex projection, Lorenz-curve fitting, incomplete beta functions, numerical integration and bisection are written in plain JavaScript in the page. Survey data, panels and microdata never leave the machine.
  2. Live where it should beFive public data APIs: the World Bank indicators and Poverty and Inequality Platform, the DHS Program, Open-Meteo and Open Library. The Court Petition Translator and Kundendu's Learn & Play also reach a translation and a speech service through this site's server: the translator sends the text of the document to Sarvam AI and Groq, and the read-aloud sends the sentence to Sarvam AI.
  3. Checked against something outsideTWFE against an explicit-dummies regression in Python; GQ poverty measures against Datt's closed forms and PIP's microdata; the wage-gap decomposition and the cost-benefit sums against independent implementations; Whipple, Myers and Benford against limiting cases; the legal calculators against published worked examples and the schedule text.
  4. Written to be readEvery tool carries a method note beside its output, and where a method or a judgment is unsettled the tool says so. Source: github.com/Varnasr/Experiments, MIT.
